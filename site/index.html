Did evolution protect the Alzheimer's genes?

Humans, mice, rats, dogs and cows split from a common ancestor tens of millions of years ago. If one stretch of a protein stayed letter-for-letter the same in all of them, it probably matters. This project checks the three genes behind inherited Alzheimer's disease for exactly that.

Chapter 1 of 7

A protein is a sentence

A gene is a recipe. The cell follows it to build a protein: a chain of building blocks called amino acids. There are 20 of them, and scientists write each one as a single letter.

This is amyloid-beta, a 42-letter piece cut from a larger protein called APP. In Alzheimer's disease it clumps into plaques in the brain.

Chapter 4 of 7

Two cuts make the problem

Why these three genes? APP is a long protein that crosses the wall of a brain cell. Two molecular scissors cut it, and the piece between the cuts is amyloid-beta.

PSEN1 and PSEN2 make the second pair of scissors. A faulty APP or a faulty pair of scissors can mean more of the sticky kind of amyloid-beta.

Step through it.

Conserved is not proof

A mutation on a conserved letter is one clue. Doctors decide what causes disease from clinical evidence.

Not a diagnosis

This compares database sequences. It cannot predict anyone's personal risk.

Five species only

A small set chosen in advance. More species would sharpen the picture.
